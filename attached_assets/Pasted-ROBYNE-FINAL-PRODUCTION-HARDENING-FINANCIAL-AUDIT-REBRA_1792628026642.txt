5. WORKSTREAM FOUR: COMPREHENSIVE FINANCIAL-FLOW AUDIT

This is one of the most important parts of the entire task.

I want a thorough, implementation-level audit of how money is handled across ROBYNE.

I do not currently have evidence that the financial system is broken. This is a preventive audit because I want to understand and verify its behavior before moving toward production.

Do not interpret this instruction as proof of an existing financial defect.

Equally, do not assume everything is correct merely because transactions appear to work in a development environment.

Audit the actual financial implementation and prove the important invariants wherever possible.

The scope is broader than payment collection.

It includes collection, transaction processing, fees, allocation, accumulation, ledger records, balances, settlement, withdrawals, reconciliation, and the relationship between these operations.

5.1 Map the complete financial architecture

First, understand the existing implementation.

Identify:

* All financial models and database tables.
* Transaction records and statuses.
* Payment provider adapters and integrations.
* Webhook handlers.
* Payment verification logic.
* Fees and commission calculations.
* Event ticket-payment flows.
* Election-related financial flows, if supported.
* Campaign contributions and fundraising flows, where supported.
* Organization balances and financial records.
* Revenue and platform-fee records.
* Withdrawal requests and processing.
* Refunds, reversals, cancellations, and chargebacks, where implemented.
* Administrative financial controls.
* Background jobs and asynchronous processing.
* Reconciliation mechanisms.
* Relevant permissions and audit logs.
* Any internal balance, ledger, wallet, or settlement mechanisms.

Trace the actual flow through the code.

Do not infer financial behavior from a dashboard, a database field name, or a successful user-interface interaction.

Produce an accurate map of the implemented financial paths before deciding what needs to change.

5.2 Audit payment collection

For each supported financial workflow, verify the full transaction lifecycle.

Investigate:

1. How a payment is initiated.
2. How the amount and currency are determined.
3. How the correct event, election, campaign, organization, or other business context is associated with the transaction.
4. How payment providers are selected.
5. How the application receives provider responses.
6. How webhook signatures and authenticity are verified, where applicable.
7. How payment completion is independently verified.
8. How pending, successful, failed, expired, cancelled, and unknown outcomes are represented.
9. How duplicate requests and repeated callbacks are handled.
10. How the application prevents a transaction from being credited or fulfilled more than once.
11. How payment records are associated with the correct customer and organization.
12. How provider fees, platform fees, taxes, and other configured deductions are calculated.
13. How the final amount is allocated and recorded.
14. What happens when a provider succeeds but the application fails to persist the result.
15. What happens when the application receives contradictory or delayed provider notifications.

Check that the application does not treat an unverified browser redirect, client-side success message, or untrusted callback as sufficient proof of payment.

Use the application’s real provider contracts and documented behavior when evaluating integrations.

Do not fabricate successful provider behavior if credentials are unavailable.

5.3 Audit withdrawal flows in particular

Withdrawal processing is a priority.

Trace the full withdrawal lifecycle from the point at which a user or organization requests a withdrawal to its final disposition.

Determine:

* Who is permitted to request a withdrawal.
* Which balances are eligible for withdrawal.
* How available balances are calculated.
* Whether pending payments, unsettled funds, reserved amounts, or other restrictions affect eligibility.
* Whether fees or minimum withdrawal amounts apply.
* How the requested amount is validated.
* How the destination account or payment details are validated.
* Whether ownership and permissions are checked appropriately.
* How withdrawal requests are recorded.
* How duplicate withdrawal requests are prevented.
* How concurrent requests are handled.
* How funds are reserved or debited.
* When a withdrawal becomes pending, approved, processing, successful, failed, rejected, or cancelled.
* Whether these states accurately reflect the actual implementation.
* How administrative approval works, if required.
* How the withdrawal is sent to the relevant provider or processed operationally.
* How provider responses and webhooks affect the withdrawal record.
* How failed withdrawals are handled.
* How reserved funds are released or restored after failure.
* How retries avoid sending money twice.
* How completed withdrawals are reconciled against the internal records.
* How users and administrators can inspect the outcome.
* How all changes are recorded for auditing.

Use the actual supported states and workflows found in the codebase. Do not add unnecessary statuses merely to make the system look more sophisticated.

Pay particular attention to the difference between:

* A withdrawal being requested.
* A withdrawal being approved.
* A withdrawal being submitted to a provider.
* A provider accepting the request.
* A withdrawal actually completing.
* A withdrawal failing and funds being restored.

These are not necessarily equivalent events.

Ensure the application does not mark money as successfully withdrawn simply because an API request was submitted or an administrator clicked an approval button.

Where a provider cannot guarantee an immediate final outcome, the application must represent that uncertainty accurately.

5.4 Verify balance and ledger correctness

Inspect the source of truth for financial balances.

Determine whether the system uses a ledger, derived balances, balance snapshots, cached values, or another existing model.

Do not assume that a particular architecture exists before inspecting the implementation.

Where financial ledgers or equivalent records exist, verify that:

* Each financial operation produces the appropriate records.
* Records are associated with the correct account, organization, and transaction.
* Amounts and currencies are consistent.
* Fees and deductions are traceable.
* Available, pending, reserved, and settled amounts are not incorrectly conflated.
* Repeated processing cannot create duplicate credits or debits.
* Failed operations are handled consistently.
* Refunds and reversals have appropriate accounting treatment.
* Balance calculations agree with the underlying records.
* Administrative changes are auditable.
* Historical transactions are not silently overwritten.
* Reconciliation can identify discrepancies.

If the application uses derived balances, verify their calculations and the mechanisms that keep them consistent.

Do not introduce a new ledger architecture simply because another design might be theoretically preferable. First determine whether the existing implementation is correct and adequate.

5.5 Audit fees, commissions, and revenue

Inspect how the application calculates and records:

* Ticket prices.
* Campaign contributions.
* Other supported collection amounts.
* Platform fees.
* Provider fees.
* Organization revenue.
* Withdrawal fees.
* Refund amounts.
* Net settlement amounts.
* Any other financial deductions or allocations.

Verify that the actual amounts charged and recorded follow the configured business rules.

Pay particular attention to:

* Integer and decimal precision.
* Currency handling.
* Rounding behavior.
* Fee calculation order.
* Minimum and maximum amounts.
* Zero or negative values.
* Boundary conditions.
* Percentage calculations.
* Fee changes over time.
* Whether historical transactions retain their original recorded financial details.

Do not assume all financial workflows use the same fee model.

Inspect the rules for each pillar and explain any intentional differences.

Where the implementation’s intended business rules are ambiguous, identify the ambiguity rather than inventing a policy.

5.6 Audit administrative financial operations

Inspect the platform administration interface and its underlying server-side permissions.

Verify that authorized administrators can perform the financial operations that the current product actually supports.

Check that:

* Financial records are accessible to the appropriate roles.
* Sensitive actions are protected by server-side authorization.
* Administrative actions are auditable.
* Approval actions cannot bypass balance restrictions.
* Withdrawal statuses cannot be manipulated through unauthorized requests.
* Sensitive provider credentials are not exposed.
* Financial summaries agree with the underlying records.
* Any manual reconciliation or correction process is appropriately controlled.

Do not add dangerous administrative shortcuts or pretend that a manual action is equivalent to a provider-confirmed financial operation.

5.7 Test financial failure and concurrency scenarios

Where feasible, write or run automated tests covering scenarios such as:

* A payment callback being delivered multiple times.
* Two requests attempting to process the same transaction.
* A provider timeout after a request has been submitted.
* A successful provider payment followed by a database failure.
* An invalid or unverifiable callback.
* Two withdrawal requests attempting to consume the same available balance.
* An administrative approval being repeated.
* A withdrawal provider request timing out.
* A withdrawal failing after funds have been reserved.
* A refund being processed more than once.
* A user attempting to access another organization’s financial records.
* Invalid amounts, currencies, and destinations.
* A financial operation being interrupted partway through processing.

These are test scenarios, not claims that such defects currently exist.

Adapt the tests to the architecture actually present.

Use isolated test data, mocks, provider sandboxes, or controlled test environments as appropriate.

Never initiate real money movement, make real withdrawals, or submit live financial transactions merely to test this task.

Do not use real customer funds or mutate production financial records.

5.8 Financial audit deliverable

Document the findings clearly.

For each major financial flow, report:

* The flow’s purpose.
* Its actual implementation path.
* The source of truth for its state and balance.
* The key calculations involved.
* The relevant security and idempotency controls.
* The tests performed.
* Any defects found.
* Any corrections implemented.
* Any unresolved limitations.

Classify findings as:

* Verified correct.
* Corrected and retested.
* Partially verified.
* Not verified because of an environmental or integration limitation.
* Defect requiring further work.

Do not give a blanket statement that the financial system is correct without supporting evidence.
