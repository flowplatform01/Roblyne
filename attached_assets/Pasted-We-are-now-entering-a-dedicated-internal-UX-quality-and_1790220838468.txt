We are now entering a dedicated internal UX quality and experience refinement stage for Evenum.

The public-facing experience is already in a good state. Do not unnecessarily redesign or disturb it.

The concern is primarily the authenticated/internal experience: user workspaces, organization operations, creation flows, management interfaces, lists, forms, navigation, platform administration and other operational areas.

Evenum was designed to be mobile-first, but parts of the internal product currently feel like desktop interfaces compressed into a mobile viewport. The result is excessive scrolling, fragmented layouts, awkward information density, unnecessary vertical length and inconsistent interaction patterns.

This requires a serious investigation—not a superficial visual refresh.

⸻

1. Investigate Before Changing

First inspect the entire authenticated/internal experience as an actual user.

Walk through:

* account creation and onboarding;
* sign-in and account setup;
* personal/user areas;
* organization creation;
* organization workspace;
* event management;
* election management;
* campaign management;
* collection/payment-related workflows;
* ticketing/registration workflows;
* candidates;
* positions;
* participants;
* lists and tables;
* creation/editing forms;
* settings;
* navigation;
* notifications;
* search/filter experiences;
* modals and sheets;
* platform administration;
* operational interfaces;
* empty states;
* loading states;
* error states.

Do not assume the existing structure is correct.

Identify where the experience feels:

* too long;
* too dense;
* unnecessarily fragmented;
* overly desktop-oriented;
* excessively scroll-heavy;
* visually chopped up;
* repetitive;
* generic;
* difficult to scan;
* difficult to understand;
* unnecessarily modal-heavy;
* unnecessarily form-heavy;
* inconsistent with other parts of Evenum.

⸻

2. Mobile-First Means Mobile-First

Re-evaluate the internal experience from the perspective of someone using a phone as their primary device, not someone using a desktop application that happens to respond to a smaller screen.

For each important workflow ask:

“If this were designed specifically for a phone first, would we structure it this way?”

Pay attention to:

* vertical space;
* thumb reach;
* action placement;
* navigation;
* content hierarchy;
* card density;
* form grouping;
* scrolling;
* fixed/sticky controls;
* bottom sheets;
* contextual actions;
* keyboard interaction;
* touch targets;
* back navigation;
* progressive disclosure.

Do not interpret mobile-first as “make everything smaller.”

It means design the information architecture and interaction model around mobile usage first, then allow the experience to expand naturally on larger screens.

Desktop should feel like an expanded version of the same intelligent experience—not the source design squeezed into mobile.

⸻

3. Reduce Unnecessary Scrolling

This is one of the major issues to investigate.

Where a page currently requires excessive scrolling, determine why.

Possible causes may include:

* unnecessary vertical spacing;
* too many independent cards;
* poor grouping;
* redundant headings;
* oversized controls;
* unnecessary descriptions;
* actions placed too far from their context;
* information that could be progressively disclosed;
* content that belongs in tabs/segments;
* controls that could be contextual;
* forms that could be intelligently grouped;
* desktop-oriented layouts being stacked vertically on mobile.

Fix the underlying information architecture rather than simply shrinking elements.

Do not compress the interface to the point where it becomes uncomfortable or difficult to use.

The objective is less unnecessary movement while preserving clarity.

⸻

4. Review Lists and Operational Screens

Pay particular attention to internal lists.

Examples include:

* candidates;
* positions;
* events;
* elections;
* campaigns;
* tickets;
* participants;
* organizations;
* transactions;
* members;
* records;
* administrative resources.

Investigate whether every item needs to occupy a large vertical card.

Where appropriate, use more efficient patterns such as:

* compact list rows;
* grouped sections;
* contextual actions;
* expandable details;
* segmented views;
* bottom sheets;
* inline actions;
* meaningful metadata hierarchy.

Do not blindly turn everything into tables.

Choose the interaction pattern that best fits the information and the device.

⸻

5. Review Creation & Editing Flows

Audit every major creation/editing experience.

Forms should feel like a coherent process rather than a long collection of unrelated fields.

Investigate:

* field grouping;
* progressive disclosure;
* conditional fields;
* unnecessary repetition;
* section hierarchy;
* action placement;
* validation;
* autosave where appropriate;
* mobile keyboard behaviour;
* navigation between sections;
* confirmation patterns.

If enabling one option reveals additional configuration, the interface should intelligently reveal only what is relevant.

Avoid making users scroll through configuration that does not apply to them.

⸻

6. Internal Navigation

Review the navigation architecture.

Determine whether users can quickly understand:

* where they are;
* what they are managing;
* what the current context is;
* how to move back;
* where important actions live;
* where settings belong.

Organization context should remain obvious.

Avoid making users repeatedly traverse long pages simply to reach common actions.

Use contextual navigation where appropriate.

Do not add navigation merely to solve poor information architecture.

⸻

7. Platform Administration & Operational Experience

Give the platform-admin and operational interfaces special attention.

These areas currently feel particularly long and fragmented.

Do not treat the admin interface as a generic dashboard.

Review it as a serious operational product.

An administrator should be able to:

* understand the current state quickly;
* find important information quickly;
* perform common actions efficiently;
* identify problems;
* navigate between related records;
* avoid unnecessary scrolling;
* understand destructive actions;
* recover from mistakes.

Use appropriate density for an operational environment without making the interface visually chaotic.

⸻

8. Consistency Without Genericity

Establish stronger internal interaction patterns across Evenum.

For example, similar concepts should behave similarly:

* creation;
* editing;
* deletion;
* confirmation;
* filtering;
* searching;
* status changes;
* contextual actions;
* navigation;
* empty states;
* loading;
* errors.

However, do not turn the application into a generic component-library-looking SaaS dashboard.

Evenum should retain its own visual and interaction language.

Avoid:

* generic dashboard layouts;
* unnecessary rounded-card grids everywhere;
* excessive pills;
* excessive shadows;
* arbitrary gradients;
* decorative UI without purpose;
* repetitive cards;
* generic AI-generated layouts.

The goal is a coherent product system, not uniformity for its own sake.

⸻

9. Preserve Existing Product Logic

This is an experience refinement, not a functional rewrite.

Do not remove or simplify important functionality merely because a screen is currently complex.

If something is complex because the underlying workflow genuinely requires complexity, improve how that complexity is presented.

Do not break:

* voting;
* elections;
* events;
* campaigns;
* collections;
* ticketing;
* payment;
* organizations;
* permissions;
* authentication;
* localization;
* PWA functionality;
* caching;
* Redis/background infrastructure;
* existing responsive behaviour.

Preserve the application’s existing business rules.

⸻

10. Think in User Journeys, Not Individual Screens

Do not optimize screens in isolation.

Trace complete journeys.

For example:

Sign up → create organization → enter workspace → create event → configure it → publish → manage it

and:

Enter organization → create election → add positions → add candidates → configure voting → publish → monitor

and:

Discover → open event/campaign → interact → register/pay/vote where applicable → receive confirmation → return later

Identify friction between screens as well as inside screens.

A screen can look beautiful and still create a poor experience if the transition into or out of it is awkward.

⸻

11. Responsive Behaviour

Test important workflows at realistic:

* small mobile widths;
* larger phones;
* tablets;
* laptop;
* desktop.

Do not merely verify that nothing overflows.

Verify that the interaction model itself adapts appropriately.

Something that works as a horizontal arrangement on desktop may need a different interaction pattern on mobile—not simply a vertical stack.

⸻

12. Use Real Product Context

Use the actual Evenum content, records and workflows when evaluating the experience.

Do not judge the interface only with empty states or artificial placeholder content.

Test with:

* short names;
* long names;
* many candidates;
* many events;
* long campaign descriptions;
* multiple positions;
* multiple statuses;
* populated organizations;
* real-looking transaction/payment states;
* different account types.

A layout that looks good with three records but becomes unusable with fifty records is not finished.

⸻

13. Visual Hierarchy & Density

The objective is not “less UI.”

The objective is better information density.

Users should immediately distinguish:

1. what this page is;
2. what requires attention;
3. what they can do;
4. what information is important;
5. what is secondary;
6. what can be opened if needed.

Reduce visual noise while improving information availability.

Important information should not be buried merely because the page is trying to look minimal.

⸻

14. Interaction Quality

Audit the small details that make an application feel mature:

* touch feedback;
* button states;
* loading behaviour;
* optimistic updates where safe;
* disabled states;
* validation;
* confirmation;
* undo/recovery where appropriate;
* keyboard behaviour;
* focus management;
* bottom-sheet behaviour;
* modal sizing;
* back behaviour;
* transitions;
* preserving scroll position;
* retaining user context after actions.

These details matter heavily on mobile.

⸻

15. Final Standard

After the investigation, make targeted improvements across the internal experience.

Do not perform a “big redesign” just to make visible changes.

Every change should answer a real UX problem discovered during the audit.

The resulting Evenum experience should feel:

* intentional;
* mobile-first;
* fast to navigate;
* information-dense without being crowded;
* calm;
* mature;
* coherent;
* professional;
* distinctly Evenum;
* efficient for repeated operational use.

The desired reaction should be:

“This is a serious product that was designed properly.”

Not:

“This is a desktop dashboard that was squeezed into a phone.”

And not:

“This looks like another AI-generated SaaS application.”

⸻

Execution Requirements

Before modifying anything:

1. Inspect the existing implementation.
2. Map the major internal user journeys.
3. Identify the highest-impact UX problems.
4. Group related problems instead of patching individual symptoms.
5. Implement the improvements systematically.
6. Test the workflows on mobile and desktop.
7. Use Playwright/browser testing where useful.
8. Test populated and edge-case states.
9. Verify that existing functionality has not regressed.
10. Review the final experience as a real mobile user would—not only as a developer looking at individual components.

Do not rebuild Evenum from scratch.

Do not replace working architecture.

Do not introduce unnecessary dependencies.

Do not make changes simply to make the application look different.

Investigate deeply, understand the existing product, then make the smallest set of high-quality architectural UX improvements that produce a substantially better internal experience.
